## flux | Text S1. Flux calculation, detection limits and quality control

All fluxes were calculated from the raw 1-Hz analyzer records with a single, reproducible procedure (scripts/2_flux/). Chamber closure was taken from the start time recorded on the field datasheet against the analyzer clock (MGGA) or from the start of the tagged measurement record (LI-7810), and the first 20 s after closure were discarded to remove the chamber-placement transient; discarding between 0 and 45 s instead changed median fluxes by at most 10%. For 121 MGGA measurements made between May and July 2024, the field team had corrected the start time after inspecting each record, and the corrected start was used without a further offset. Each window ended at the recorded end time, or earlier where an abrupt, sustained fall in CO₂ showed that the chamber had been removed (12 measurements). Median window length was 160 s (MGGA) and 171 s (LI-7810).

Fluxes were calculated as F = (slope × n) / A, where F is the flux (nmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for CH₄; µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for CO₂), slope is the rate of change of the dry mole fraction, n is the total moles of gas in the closed system, calculated with the ideal gas law from atmospheric pressure and air temperature at the Fisher Meteorological Station (Boose and VanScoy 2025a), and A is the bark area enclosed by the collar (a cylindrical section 10.16 cm in diameter). Collar depth was measured at four interior locations and averaged to calculate each tree's collar volume.

The closed-system volume was the measured collar volume, cap and tubing plus 0.028 L of analyzer internal volume for both analyzers (the LI-7810 sample volume; the GLA131 cavity is similar, about 25 cm³). Linear and non-linear (Hutchinson–Mosier) models were fitted with the goFlux R package (Rheault et al. 2024), and the better estimate was selected with goFlux's best.flux criteria, which bound the curvature parameter following Hüppi et al. (2018) and use the linear estimate when the non-linear estimate exceeds it by more than a factor of two. The non-linear model was selected for 72% of MGGA and 88% of LI-7810 measurements. Ninety-nine measurements had no archived 1-Hz record (the October 2025 campaign, one day in May 2024 and six closures in September 2023); their fluxes are linear fits over the recorded window.

For each measurement we calculated a minimum detectable flux (MDF) as MDF = 1.96 σ / t × n / A (95%, two-sided), where t is the closure length in seconds and σ is the analyzer precision on that day, estimated as the median absolute deviation of first differences over the full raw record of the analyzer for that day, divided by √2 (fluxqc R package). Estimating σ per analyzer and day accommodates the change of analyzer, drift over time and any change in logging interval. Median precision was 3.9 ppb CH₄ for the MGGA (range 2.9–6.9 ppb among days) and 0.14 ppb CH₄ for the LI-7810 (0.11–0.26 ppb). Fluxes below the MDF were retained and flagged, because removing them biases means upward.

Every analyzer-specific quantity (units, water-vapour correction, window definition, clock, daily precision and MDF, and system volume) was handled per analyzer. The two analyzers were not run side by side, so a systematic offset between them cannot be separated from differences between 2023–2024 and 2025.

Automated checks of every CO₂ and CH₄ trace flagged measurements for inspection: CO₂ not increasing during a growing-season closure (indicating a poor seal), CO₂ falling or dropping within the window, a delayed rise, start-of-window transients, CH₄ steps, a starting concentration well above ambient, and records noisier than the analyzer's typical precision (scripts/2_flux/03_trace_qc.R). The 55 measurements with the clearest problems were inspected individually: 50 were kept as fitted, two were refitted over a manually chosen window, and three were excluded because of a concentration spike at the start of the record. No other measurements were excluded. Flags are provided with the published data.

## models | Text S2. Model selection and diagnostics

Among the predictors significant in the window screening, we identified highly correlated pairs (|r| ≥ 0.7) and removed series that duplicated a retained measurement of the same quantity (soil temperature from a second tower and from the Fisher station, and one latent-heat series), choosing by measurement relevance rather than by fit. The remaining significant predictors other than the two core drivers were screened by greedy forward selection, adding at each step the predictor that most increased the marginal R² of a mixed model containing the core predictors and species, up to six predictors. Each of these six was then tested as an addition to the core interaction model, alone and interacting with species, by likelihood-ratio test; those that lowered the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) by more than 2 were added together, and the extended model was retained only if it also lowered the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Out-of-sample predictive skill was assessed by leaving out one sampling date at a time (refitting the model and predicting the omitted date, including the tree random effect) and one tree at a time (fixed effects only), and by fitting the models to 2023–2024 and predicting 2025, restricted to observations within the 2023–2024 range of the predictors. The same procedures were applied to a model with species alone. Accuracy is reported as root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and mean bias (predicted minus observed), on the asinh scale and after back-transformation to flux units. Because the back-transform of a mean on the asinh scale underestimates the mean flux, we also report the ratio of mean predicted to mean observed flux, with and without a smearing correction (Duan 1983) (Table {T:diagnostics}).

Because soil temperature is strongly seasonal, we regressed the temperature predictor on the seasonal cycle (sine and cosine of day of year), replaced temperature by its fitted seasonal cycle, and tested whether the departures of temperature from the seasonal cycle explained additional variation (likelihood-ratio test). We also compared the core model with a model in which temperature was replaced by the seasonal harmonics themselves, and with alternative core models substituting soil water content for water table or latent heat flux for temperature, all on the observations with every driver available (Table {T:wet_comp}).

The effect of the sampling-date random effect was assessed by refitting each final model without it. Sensitivity to the response transformation was assessed by refitting the final models with asinh(flux/0.1) (more log-like), asinh(flux/10) (more linear) and rank-normal scores; the N. sylvatica effects were significant under every transformation, whereas the A. rubrum effects were not. For the window screening, the maximum |r| over all windows for each model driver was compared with a null distribution from 500 permutations in which environmental records were reassigned among sampling dates and the full window search repeated (Table {T:diagnostics}). All diagnostics are produced by scripts/5_models/06_model_checks.R.

Results. Wetland core model: leaving out one sampling date at a time, the predictive R² was 0.71 (species alone 0.45), with an RMSE of 0.49 on the asinh scale and a mean bias of −0.03. Back-transformed, the mean absolute error was 1.2 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and predictions underestimated the mean flux by about half (mean predicted/observed 0.52; 0.57 with a smearing correction, Duan 1983), because the largest emissions, from a few N. sylvatica trees on a few dates, were underpredicted. Of the variance not explained by the fixed effects, 43% reflected persistent differences among individual trees, 12% conditions shared by all trees on a sampling date and 46% residual variation; the tree share is larger than the repeatability values in the main text because the drivers account for much of the temporal variation that dominates the fluxes. Soil temperature followed the seasonal cycle closely (the cycle explained 94% of its variation), but departures of temperature from its seasonal cycle explained additional variation in flux (χ² = 22.0, df = 3, p < 0.001), and replacing temperature by its seasonal cycle worsened the fit (ΔAIC = +53). On the 654 observations with every driver available (July 2023–August 2025), a more flexible seasonal model (season × water table × species, 18 parameters) did not fit better than the core model (ΔAIC = +0.3, ΔBIC = +27), and alternative core models substituting soil water content for water table, or latent heat flux for temperature, fitted worse (ΔAIC = +18 and +194; Table {T:wet_comp}). Adding the two predictors that individually improved the core model (deep soil water content and throughfall, each interacting with species) raised R² only to 60.0% (vs. 59.5% on the same observations) and lowered AIC (−11) but raised BIC (+16), so the core model was retained; the N. sylvatica temperature, water-table and interaction effects were nearly unchanged in the extended model (Table {T:slopes}).

Upland models: Model B (the wetland predictors and windows) and Model A (instantaneous upland soil temperature and soil water content) fitted the 721 observations they share about equally (ΔAIC = 2.3 in favour of Model B; Table {T:upland}). Neither predicted omitted sampling dates better than species alone (predictive R² 0.07 for both, vs. 0.07–0.08; RMSE 0.14 on the asinh scale, MAE 0.08 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹, mean predicted/observed 0.90, or 0.99 with a smearing correction).

Variance partitioning: partitioning the variance of asinh-transformed flux among nested random intercepts (shares within each model sum to 100%), site explained 23.5% of the variance of all observations, and wetland variance was about 800-fold higher than upland variance. At the wetland, species identity explained 30.7% of variance, individual trees within species 6.8%, and residual (largely temporal, within-tree) variation 62.5%. At the upland, residual variation dominated (90.1%), with minimal contributions from species identity (1.4%) or individual trees (8.5%).

## tomo | Text S3. Tomography

Sonic and electrical resistance tomography were measured at breast height (1.37 m) with the PiCUS system (Argus Electronic GmbH, Rostock, Germany); the procedures, image analysis and validation are described in full in the companion tomography study (Thompson et al. 2026).

Sonic tomography (SoT) detects internal decay by measuring acoustic wave velocity through wood (Brazee et al. 2011). A minimum of 8 sensors were placed around the trunk circumference following manufacturer-recommended spacing. Sterilized steel nails were inserted through bark to contact wood, and sensor positions were recorded using the PiCUS Caliper 3, which determines cross-sectional geometry through triangulation. Each point was struck 3–5 times with an electronic hammer connected to the SoT module, which records acoustic travel times between all sensor pairs. The PiCUS Q74 software generates cross-sectional images of apparent sonic velocity via tomographic reconstruction. Decayed wood transmits sound more slowly due to reduced modulus of elasticity and density; the software compares measured travel times to expected times through solid wood within the same trunk, facilitating cross-species comparisons (Gilbert et al. 2016). Tomograms display brown (high velocity, sound wood), green (intermediate velocity, reduced density), and blue/violet (low velocity, advanced decay or cavity). The Q74 software exports percentages of nondecayed (brown pixels) and decaying (non-brown pixels) wood.

Electrical resistance tomography (ERT) measures the spatial distribution of electrical resistance across the cross-section (Brazee et al. 2011). Resistance is influenced by moisture content, ion concentration, and cell structure: low resistance indicates elevated moisture or ions often associated with active fungal decay or bacterial wetwood; high resistance indicates dry wood or cavities. We used the TreeTronic 3 Tomograph, attaching electrode clamps to the same nails used for SoT. The system sequentially injects current between electrode pairs and measures voltage distribution, reconstructing spatial resistivity patterns (Ω·m). The PiCUS software does not provide quantitative ERT summary statistics, so we developed a custom image analysis application in R using the Shiny framework (Chang et al. 2026) to extract numeric metrics from exported images. The application extracts the colorbar, constructs a log-transformed calibration function relating pixel colors to resistivity, and applies this calibration to pixels within a user-delineated cross-sectional boundary. For each tree we calculated eight metrics: mean and median resistivity, standard deviation, coefficient of variation (CV), Gini coefficient, Shannon entropy, central moisture accumulation and radial gradient.
