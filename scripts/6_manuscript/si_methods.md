## flux | Text S1. Flux calculation, detection limits and quality control

All fluxes were calculated from the raw 1-Hz analyzer records with a single, reproducible procedure (scripts/2_flux/). Chamber closure was taken from the start time recorded on the field datasheet against the analyzer clock (MGGA) or from the start of the tagged measurement record (LI-7810), and the first 20 s after closure were discarded to remove the chamber-placement transient; discarding between 0 and 45 s instead changed median fluxes by at most 10%. For 121 MGGA measurements made between May and July 2024, the field team had corrected the start time after inspecting each record, and the corrected start was used without a further offset. Each window ended at the recorded end time, or earlier where an abrupt, sustained fall in CO₂ showed that the chamber had been removed (13 measurements). Median window length was 160 s (MGGA) and 172 s (LI-7810).

Fluxes were calculated as F = (slope × n) / A, where F is the flux (nmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for CH₄; µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for CO₂), slope is the rate of change of the dry mole fraction, n is the total moles of gas in the closed system, calculated with the ideal gas law from atmospheric pressure and air temperature at the Fisher Meteorological Station (Boose and VanScoy 2025a), and A is the bark area enclosed by the collar (a cylindrical section 10.16 cm in diameter). Collar depth was measured at four interior locations and averaged to calculate each tree's collar volume.

The closed-system volume was the measured collar volume, cap and tubing plus 0.028 L of analyzer internal volume for both analyzers (the LI-7810 sample volume; the GLA131 cavity is similar, about 25 cm³). Linear and non-linear (Hutchinson–Mosier) models were fitted with the goFlux R package (Rheault et al. 2024), and the better estimate was selected with goFlux's best.flux criteria, which bound the curvature parameter following Hüppi et al. (2018) and use the linear estimate when the non-linear estimate exceeds it by more than a factor of two. The non-linear model was selected for 73% of MGGA and 89% of LI-7810 measurements. Ninety-nine measurements had no archived 1-Hz record (the October 2025 campaign, one day in May 2024 and six closures in September 2023); their fluxes are linear fits over the recorded window.

For each measurement we calculated a minimum detectable flux (MDF) as MDF = 1.96 σ / t × n / A, where t is the closure length in seconds and σ is the analyzer precision on that day, estimated for each analyzer and field day as the median, over that day's closures, of the MAD-based standard deviation of the second differences of the concentration within the fitting window, divided by √6 (goFlux empirical.prec; Gewirtzman 2026a). Second differences remove the flux trend, so the estimate is not inflated by the concentration rise itself. The factor 1.96 is a benchmark multiplier on concentration noise rather than a calibrated 95% detection test; the MDF describes the precision of a measurement, whereas the confidence interval of the fitted slope describes the uncertainty of that flux estimate (Cowan et al. 2025). Estimating σ per analyzer and day accommodates the change of analyzer, drift over time and any change in logging interval. Median precision was 3.5 ppb CH₄ for the MGGA (range 2.6–5.4 ppb among days) and 0.10 ppb CH₄ for the LI-7810 (0.08–0.11 ppb). All fluxes were retained, including negative fluxes and those smaller in magnitude than the MDF, and are flagged rather than removed: discarding small fluxes would bias means away from zero and remove most upland measurements, and the MDF describes the precision of a measurement, not whether the tree emits CH₄. Users of the published data can apply their own thresholds to these flags.

Every analyzer-specific quantity (units, water-vapour correction, window definition, clock, daily precision and MDF, and system volume) was handled per analyzer. The two analyzers were not run side by side, so a systematic offset between them cannot be separated from differences between 2023–2024 and 2025.

Automated checks of every CO₂ and CH₄ trace flagged measurements with CO₂ not increasing during a growing-season closure (indicating a poor seal), CO₂ falling or dropping within the window, a delayed rise, start-of-window transients, CH₄ steps, a starting concentration well above ambient, and records noisier than the analyzer's typical precision (scripts/2_flux/03_trace_qc.R). These flags are provided with the published data; they do not remove measurements.

## models | Text S2. Model selection and diagnostics

Among the predictors significant in the window screening, we identified highly correlated pairs (|r| ≥ 0.7) and removed series that duplicated a retained measurement of the same quantity (soil temperature from a second tower and from the Fisher station, and one latent-heat series), choosing by measurement relevance rather than by fit. The remaining significant predictors other than the two core drivers were screened by greedy forward selection, adding at each step the predictor that most increased the marginal R² of a mixed model containing the core predictors and species, up to six predictors. These were then tested as an addition to the core interaction model, alone and interacting with species, by likelihood-ratio test; those that lowered the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) by more than 2 were added together, and the extended model was retained only if it also lowered the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Out-of-sample predictive skill was assessed by leaving out one sampling date at a time (refitting the model and predicting the omitted date, including the tree random effect) and one tree at a time (fixed effects only), and by fitting the models to 2023–2024 and predicting 2025, restricted to observations within the 2023–2024 range of the predictors. The same procedures were applied to a model with species alone. Accuracy is reported as root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and mean bias (predicted minus observed), on the asinh scale and after back-transformation to flux units. Because the back-transform of a mean on the asinh scale underestimates the mean flux, we also report the ratio of mean predicted to mean observed flux, with and without a smearing correction (Duan 1983) (Table {T:diagnostics}).

Because soil temperature is strongly seasonal, we regressed the temperature predictor on the seasonal cycle (sine and cosine of day of year), replaced temperature by its fitted seasonal cycle, and tested whether the departures of temperature from the seasonal cycle explained additional variation (likelihood-ratio test). We also compared the core model with a model in which temperature was replaced by the seasonal harmonics themselves, and with alternative core models substituting soil water content for water table or latent heat flux for temperature, all on the observations with every driver available (Table {T:wet_comp}).

The effect of the sampling-date random effect was assessed by refitting each final model without it. Sensitivity to the response transformation was assessed by refitting the final models with asinh(flux) and asinh(flux/10) (both more linear than the main scale) and rank-normal scores; the N. sylvatica effects were significant under every transformation; the wetland A. rubrum and T. canadensis temperature effects were not significant on the most linear scale, the upland A. rubrum temperature effect was significant under every transformation, and the upland Q. rubra temperature effect only on the main and rank-normal scales. All diagnostics are produced by scripts/5_models/06_model_checks.R.

Results. Wetland core model: leaving out one sampling date at a time, the predictive R² was 0.72 (species alone 0.49), with an RMSE of 0.89 on the asinh(flux / 0.1) scale and a mean bias of −0.06. Back-transformed, the mean absolute error was 1.0 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and predictions underestimated the mean flux by about half (mean predicted/observed 0.51; 0.68 with a smearing correction, Duan 1983), because the largest emissions, from a few N. sylvatica trees on a few dates, were underpredicted. Of the variance not explained by the fixed effects, 36% reflected persistent differences among individual trees, 7% conditions shared by all trees on a sampling date and 57% residual variation; the tree share is larger than the repeatability values in the main text because the drivers account for much of the temporal variation that dominates the fluxes. Soil temperature followed the seasonal cycle closely (the cycle explained 96% of its variation), and departures of temperature from its seasonal cycle did not explain significant additional variation in flux (χ² = 4.7, df = 3, p = 0.20), so the temperature effect cannot be separated from other warm-season processes; replacing temperature by its seasonal cycle nonetheless worsened the fit (ΔAIC = +9.9). On the 747 observations with every driver available (July 2023–August 2025), a more flexible seasonal model (season × water table × species, 18 parameters) did not fit better than the core model (ΔAIC = −1.2, ΔBIC = +26), and alternative core models substituting soil water content for water table, or latent heat flux for temperature, fitted worse (ΔAIC = +8.6 and +107; Table {T:wet_comp}). Adding the four predictors that individually improved the core model (deep and shallow soil water content, each interacting with species, relative humidity and friction velocity) raised R² only to 60.7% (vs. 60.2% on the same observations) and raised both AIC (+6.5) and BIC (+43), so the core model was retained; the N. sylvatica temperature, water-table and interaction effects remained positive and significant in the extended model (Table {T:slopes}).

Upland models: Model B (the wetland predictors and windows) and Model A (instantaneous upland soil temperature and soil water content) fitted the 866 observations they share about equally (ΔAIC = 1.7 in favour of Model A; Table {T:upland}). Neither predicted omitted sampling dates appreciably better than species alone (predictive R² 0.14 for both, vs. 0.12 for species alone; RMSE 0.69 on the asinh(flux / 0.1) scale, MAE 0.07 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹, mean predicted/observed 0.69, or 0.90–0.91 with a smearing correction).

Variance partitioning: partitioning the variance of asinh-transformed flux among nested random intercepts (shares within each model sum to 100%), site explained 29.4% of the variance of all observations, and the variance of untransformed flux was about 870-fold higher at the wetland than at the upland. At the wetland, species identity explained 46.2% of variance, individual trees within species 12.8%, and residual (largely temporal, within-tree) variation 41.0%. At the upland, residual variation dominated (83.8%), with minimal contributions from species identity (4.3%) or individual trees (12.0%).

## tomo | Text S3. Tomography

Sonic and electrical resistance tomography were measured at breast height (1.37 m) with the PiCUS system (Argus Electronic GmbH, Rostock, Germany); the procedures, image analysis and validation are described in full in the companion tomography study (Thompson et al. 2026).

Sonic tomography (SoT) detects internal decay by measuring acoustic wave velocity through wood (Brazee et al. 2011). A minimum of 8 sensors were placed around the trunk circumference following manufacturer-recommended spacing. Sterilized steel nails were inserted through bark to contact wood, and sensor positions were recorded using the PiCUS Caliper 3, which determines cross-sectional geometry through triangulation. Each point was struck 3–5 times with an electronic hammer connected to the SoT module, which records acoustic travel times between all sensor pairs. The PiCUS Q74 software generates cross-sectional images of apparent sonic velocity via tomographic reconstruction. Decayed wood transmits sound more slowly due to reduced modulus of elasticity and density; the software compares measured travel times to expected times through solid wood within the same trunk, facilitating cross-species comparisons (Gilbert et al. 2016). Tomograms display brown (high velocity, sound wood), green (intermediate velocity, reduced density), and blue/violet (low velocity, advanced decay or cavity). The Q74 software exports percentages of nondecayed (brown pixels) and decaying (non-brown pixels) wood.

Electrical resistance tomography (ERT) measures the spatial distribution of electrical resistance across the cross-section (Brazee et al. 2011). Resistance is influenced by moisture content, ion concentration, and cell structure: low resistance indicates elevated moisture or ions often associated with active fungal decay or bacterial wetwood; high resistance indicates dry wood or cavities. We used the TreeTronic 3 Tomograph, attaching electrode clamps to the same nails used for SoT. The system sequentially injects current between electrode pairs and measures voltage distribution, reconstructing spatial resistivity patterns (Ω·m). The PiCUS software does not provide quantitative ERT summary statistics, so we developed a custom image analysis application in R (Shiny; Thompson and Gewirtzman 2026) to extract numeric metrics from exported images. The application extracts the colorbar, constructs a log-transformed calibration function relating pixel colors to resistivity, and applies this calibration to pixels within a user-delineated cross-sectional boundary. For each tree we calculated eight metrics: mean and median resistivity, standard deviation, coefficient of variation (CV), Gini coefficient, Shannon entropy, central moisture accumulation and radial gradient.
